### Problem (summary paragraph)

The BSH-Elevator ESP32 node could not stay connected to the network, repeatedly cycling through a boot-reconnect loop (the OpenPLC container logged an alternating pattern of "Operation now in progress," "Connection refused," and brief "Connected" states followed immediately by "Connection timed out"). The root cause was traced to inadequate power delivery, not a faulty board or weak Wi-Fi signal: the node was being powered through the BSH-PowerBox relay rail, whose contact (COM) was fed from the PowerBox ESP32's own VIN pin. Because the elevator is the heaviest node in the system — its DC motor shares the same 5V rail as the ESP32, and both draw current through the relay contacts and breadboard jumpers from an already-limited source — the voltage sagged below the level the ESP32's regulator needs whenever the motor ran or Wi-Fi transmitted, causing the board to brown out and reboot before it could finish joining the network. This was confirmed by substitution: the elevator ran perfectly stably when powered directly from a USB source, but failed immediately when returned to the PowerBox relay rail, with nothing else changed.

### Solution (summary paragraph — chosen, not yet bench-confirmed)

The solution keeps the BSH-PowerBox in full control of the elevator's power — preserving the cascade rules and the "attacker cuts power" demonstration — while fixing the weak power source behind it. The PowerBox relay stays exactly as wired on its control side: its coil is still driven by the PowerBox ESP32's GPIO 25, so the PowerBox still decides when the elevator is powered on or cut off, and SCADA still sees the elevator appear and disappear accordingly. The only change is where the switched power comes from. Instead of feeding the relay's COM contact from the PowerBox ESP32's own VIN pin (the weak, current-limited path that caused the brownouts), COM is re-routed to a dedicated 5V 2A phone charger. The relay's NO contact continues to feed the elevator's 5V pin, and the charger's ground is tied into the system's common ground rail so every device shares one reference. In effect, the PowerBox keeps flipping the switch, but a dedicated supply now sits behind that switch, able to deliver the steady current the elevator's ESP32 and its shared motor rail demand. The fix will be validated on the bench by measuring the voltage at the elevator's 5V pin while the motor runs — it must hold at roughly 4.7V or above — and confirming the elevator joins Wi-Fi and stays connected (Status 0 in Ignition), after which the end-to-end cascade will be re-tested so that pressing the BSH fire-alarm pull station physically powers the elevator down.
